# The Supermarket Theory of Building an Agency

By Ambrissh · Thruput Labs · 6 November 2026

When someone asks me what the moat of a service agency is, I usually want to ask them something back: why do you shop at your particular supermarket?

It probably isn’t the only place selling milk, rice or toothpaste. Another shop down the road may have almost the same shelves. You still have a reason to walk into this one. It’s close. You know where things are. The prices make sense. The person at the counter remembers you. You trust that you’ll get what you came for.

That’s how I think about building an agency.

I’m interested in a business that people find, trust and pay to solve a useful problem. That can be a perfectly worthwhile ambition before anyone starts talking about a revolutionary moat.

## There’s room for more than one shop

In agency conversations, I sometimes feel as though we skip straight to defending a business we haven’t built yet.

What stops someone from offering the same service? What’s proprietary? Why can’t a competitor use the same AI tools?

Those are fair questions. But there’s another question I want to answer first: can I find a group of customers, understand what they need, deliver the work properly and make a profit doing it?

A local supermarket doesn’t need every household in the city. It needs enough people who have a reason to shop there. In the same way, I don’t need every business that needs a website or an automation. I need a manageable, repeatable way to reach the ones I can serve well.

That is the part of the supermarket analogy that matters to me. Similar services can coexist because customers have different circumstances and different reasons for choosing someone.

## For an agency, “location” means being within reach

The comparison has a limit. A supermarket has a physical location. An online agency can be compared with someone across the country in a few seconds.

So I have to build my own version of being the shop around the corner.

That could mean knowing a particular kind of business, being part of a local network, answering questions where potential clients already spend time, or being recommended by someone they trust. It can also mean simply being easy to contact and clear about what I can deliver.

A business owner with a messy billing process may not be looking for the most famous software agency. They may be looking for someone who understands the problem, gives them a sensible scope and actually replies when they have a question.

Being accessible is part of the offer. It’s something I have to earn through how I work, rather than write into a tagline and forget about.

## The client is buying a job done properly

In my case, the work can be a website, a custom workflow or an automation. The client’s starting point is usually much more ordinary than those categories suggest.

They need their services explained clearly. They want an invoice without retyping the same customer details. They need to know which event a staff member is assigned to. They want a customer to reach WhatsApp with the right product already in the message.

I can use the same tools as another agency and still make different decisions about those jobs. The questions I ask, the scope I propose, the interface I build and the checks I do all affect what the client receives.

That’s where I want to put my attention. A good-looking demo is a useful beginning. The delivered work still has to make sense on an ordinary working day, in the hands of the person who uses it.

I also want to be clear about what I’m selling. A finished website is a deliverable. More sales depend on other things too: the offer, pricing, demand, follow-up and how the business operates. I should be responsible for the work I agree to do, and honest about what that work can establish.

## A deal can bring someone in. The experience gives them a reason to return

Supermarkets run offers. Agencies do too, whether that’s an introductory project, a small fixed scope or a price that makes a first engagement easier to try.

I see nothing wrong with making it easier for someone to become a customer. I do want the deal to make sense for both sides.

If I quote a project cheaply and quietly assume it will take one evening, every extra revision becomes a problem. If the client thinks “a website” includes every future idea they have, the same price means something very different to each of us.

A clear scope matters even for a small project. What am I building? What does the client need to supply? What counts as finished? How will changes and support be handled?

For me, a sensible margin starts there. I need to count my time, the tools the work needs, revisions and the support I’ve agreed to provide. Revenue on its own doesn’t tell me whether the job was good business.

A small project can be worth doing. It still needs a boundary.

## What I really mean when I say “good clients”

I’ve described good clients as a possible moat. What I mean more precisely is the relationship that can develop when the work goes well.

A client knows how I communicate. I understand their business. We have a clearer starting point for the next job. If they introduce me to another owner, that person arrives with some trust already in place.

None of that happens automatically. A happy client may never need another project. Someone may recommend me months later, or not at all. I can ask for feedback and referrals, but I can’t treat them as guaranteed income.

Still, repeat work and word of mouth are worth building towards. I’d like the next conversation to begin with “you helped us with this before”, rather than have to make a completely new pitch every time.

I also want clients to stay because the service is useful and dependable. Clear documentation, sensible access and a proper handover belong in that relationship. Making a client afraid to leave is a poor substitute for giving them a reason to come back.

## The supermarket does have advantages. That doesn’t break the analogy

I wouldn’t literally claim that supermarkets have no competitive advantages. A convenient location, familiar service, efficient operations and supplier relationships can all matter. Some of those advantages are difficult to reproduce.

My point is about the starting ambition. A business can begin with a familiar service and earn its place by serving a particular set of people well.

There’s also a difference between operating a profitable agency and building a software product designed to scale much further. A SaaS product has its own costs, product decisions and ongoing obligations. I don’t want to pretend that a custom project and a subscription product are the same business just because both involve software.

If I want to grow the agency, I’ll need to make delivery less dependent on me, keep the quality consistent and understand where the next client comes from. The supermarket comparison gives me a way to explain the opportunity. It doesn’t excuse me from doing that work.

## The numbers I want to watch

I want to know how people found us, which enquiries were a good fit, how much effort it took to win a project and how much time the agreed work actually required.

Then I want to know what happened afterwards. Did the client use what we built? Were there unresolved issues? Did support take more time than expected? Did they return or introduce someone else?

Those questions help me judge whether the business is becoming more dependable. They also tell me when I need to change the offer, raise a price, tighten a scope or stop taking a particular kind of project.

A market can have plenty of demand and still be difficult for my business to reach profitably. I need to test my own way of reaching it.

## The business I’m trying to build

At Thruput Labs, I want to be easy to find, straightforward to deal with and reliable in the work we deliver. I want the website, software or workflow to feel useful to the person who paid for it.

That doesn’t require me to invent an entirely new category of agency. It requires me to understand a customer, price the work sensibly, deliver it and give them a reason to trust me with the next thing.

> I don’t need every customer. I need a small, repeatable share of the demand.

That’s my supermarket theory. Be within reach. Stock something people need. Make the experience good enough that they remember you the next time they need it.
