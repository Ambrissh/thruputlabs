# Vibe Coding for Search: My AI-Native SEO and GEO Playbook

By Ambrissh · Thruput Labs · 6 November 2026

An AI can build a convincing first version of an app before anyone has worked out what should happen when a customer changes their mind.

The screens exist. The buttons look right. The happy path works. Then someone uses it differently, and a missing part of the workflow becomes very obvious.

I think AI-native SEO and GEO can have the same problem. A page can read beautifully and still answer the wrong question. An audit can sound confident without being based on anything the client’s customers actually search for.

That’s the analogy I keep coming back to: using AI for search is a lot like using AI to build software. It can accelerate the work enormously. The person directing it still needs to understand the job and check what comes out.

## The problem starts when the output becomes the brief

When people say “vibe coding”, they can mean different things. Here, I mean asking AI to build something, accepting what appears and carrying on without understanding the decisions underneath it.

Imagine asking for a billing tool. The AI produces a neat invoice screen, and you assume the job is finished. But can someone save a draft, correct a mistake, find an old bill or export the invoice they actually need? The screen alone doesn’t answer those questions.

Now imagine asking AI to “optimise this website for SEO and GEO”. You get keywords, a page outline and twenty FAQ ideas. It looks like a deliverable. But are those the right services, the right location and the right questions? Can search engines access the page? Has the client supplied the facts the draft now presents so confidently?

I wouldn’t accept a working-looking invoice screen as proof that the billing workflow is complete. I don’t want to accept a polished article as proof that the search problem is solved either.

## AI-native development gives me a better way to think about it

The useful part of building with AI begins when I understand what I’m trying to build.

I can describe the user, the task, the information involved and the points where the current process breaks. Then I can use AI to help implement a specific improvement and test whether it behaves as intended.

That’s the part I want to carry into SEO and GEO.

SEO means search engine optimisation. GEO means generative engine optimisation, improving how a business and its information can be discovered or used in AI-generated search answers. AEO, or answer engine optimisation, overlaps with that work by focusing on clear, useful answers.

I see a common responsibility across all of them: understand the business, give the information a useful structure and check whether people can find and use it. Calling the process “AI-native” doesn’t remove that responsibility.

## First understand what the business needs to happen

For a workflow project, I ask where the work starts and what a successful handoff looks like. An enquiry arrives. Someone responds. Information gets recorded. A task moves to the next person.

For search work, I’d start just as practically. Which services does the client want enquiries for? Which locations can they serve? What does a prospective customer need to know before they contact the business?

The client’s keyword ideas are a useful starting conversation. I’d check those ideas against the evidence available, including Search Console queries, Business Profile performance, the current search results and real customer questions.

A boutique owner might describe a service one way while a customer asks about fitting, turnaround time or whether they can bring their own fabric. I want to understand that difference before generating another page.

It’s the search equivalent of watching how someone actually creates an invoice before designing their new invoice screen.

## Then look at where the current workflow breaks

In software, I look for the step where information gets lost, repeated or misunderstood.

In SEO and GEO, I’d look for the question the current website fails to answer, the service buried in vague copy, or the relevant page that isn’t being found. I’d also inspect the technical basics rather than assume the problem is always the writing.

I want to open the pages appearing in search and being cited in AI answers. What do they explain clearly? Which claims do they support? What does the client know that those pages leave out?

That gives me a gap worth working on. It also gives me a reason to reject some of the AI’s suggestions. If the business doesn’t offer a service, a competitor mentioning it isn’t a reason to add it to the client’s website.

Copying every visible feature of another app rarely explains the workflow behind it. Copying every heading from a cited page has the same weakness.

## This is where I want AI to accelerate the work

Once the problem is specific, the AI can have a specific job.

It might help organise verified service information, compare a page with a fixed set of customer questions, suggest a clearer structure or draft wording that I can check with the client. It can also challenge the plan and point out assumptions I’ve missed.

I was interested in using Grok partly because I like a tool that pushes back. I can use that preference without making the model’s personality the method. A critical answer still needs something real to criticise.

The same goes for development. I’d rather ask AI to help fix a clearly identified handoff than ask it to “make the whole business smarter”. A narrower job gives me an output I can inspect.

For a service page, that means checking every fact: prices, availability, qualifications, service areas, photographs and the actual enquiry process. Google’s [guidance on generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) also stresses review and accuracy, and warns against producing pages at scale without adding value.

Speed is useful when I know where I’m going. Otherwise, it can just help me publish the wrong thing faster.

## A test that passes once is still only one test

Software has its own ways of misleading us. A flow can work with perfect input and fail with an empty field, a slow connection or a returning customer.

Search needs its own checks. Before changing a site, I’d save a small, repeatable list of service and customer queries. I’d record the platform, date, relevant settings, whether the client appeared and which URLs were actually cited.

After the work is published and accessible, I’d revisit the same questions under similar conditions. I’d compare those observations with search performance and the enquiries the client is receiving.

An AI answer can change between checks. A mention is different from a citation, and neither proves that a customer booked. Google also notes that the responses and links in its AI search features can vary. Its [AI features guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) says the established SEO fundamentals remain relevant, without special AI markup requirements.

For me, the useful comparison is between a saved starting point and a series of later observations. One impressive answer is the search equivalent of one successful demo.

## I don’t need to confuse a simple workflow with a complete one

I’d like to keep the process small enough to run properly. That can mean beginning without an Ahrefs or Semrush subscription when a focused audit can use the client’s data, direct page checks and recorded search observations.

It doesn’t mean a chatbot replaces every research tool. If a project needs deeper backlink work, wider keyword data or larger-scale monitoring, I need a way to answer those questions too.

The same distinction exists in development. A simple tool can solve a specific job well. Calling it simple doesn’t excuse a missing save button, a broken handoff or an untested assumption.

I want a process I can explain to the client: here’s what we found, here’s what we changed, here’s what we observed afterwards and here’s what we still don’t know.

## The analogy I want to test

The part of AI-native development that interests me is the combination of understanding and speed. Someone owns the workflow. AI helps move the implementation along. The result gets checked in use.

That is also the approach I want to bring to SEO and GEO at Thruput Labs. Start with the business and its customers, identify a useful gap, use AI to accelerate the work and keep a record of what happens next.

I’m not treating the analogy as proof that the method will produce rankings or citations. The work still has to earn that conclusion.

If understanding the workflow makes AI-built software more dependable, could the same approach make AI-native SEO and GEO work for the businesses that need them?
